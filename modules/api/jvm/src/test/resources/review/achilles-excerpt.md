# Achilles (excerpt)

Latacz (2004) argues against an Aeolian origin for this material. The target of his argument is West's 1988 article, which placed the coining of the phrase "away to sacred Ilios" after the Aeolian migration to Lesbos [[latacz2004_troy]]. Citing Nigel Spencer's archaeology, Latacz dates Greek settlement of Lesbos no earlier than 1050 BCE. The *Iliad* has Achilles sack Lesbos as part of Priam's realm, and Latacz argues that this "cannot derive from a time when Lesbos was already part of Greece", so the material predates 1050 BCE [[latacz2004_troy]]. He does not deny an Aeolian stage. He quotes Richard Janko (1992) that "Achilleus is an Aeolian hero" and that Aeolian bards "recited tales about a war at Troy", and reports the dialectologists' view that Aeolian may continue the speech of the Mycenaeans. His disagreement with West is over date: on his reading the Lesbos material was already in bardic poetry on the Greek mainland before 1050 BCE [[latacz2004_troy]]. West restated the Aeolian derivation in 2011, seven years after Latacz's English edition [[west2011_making]]. The Cambridge Guide calls the traditions of Achilles' activities in the Troad outside Troy "a complex set", to which the *Iliad* alludes as well-known stories [[pache2020_homerguide]].

Bachvarova (2016) directly challenges Latacz’s reconstruction of the poem’s political geography. She accepts some Bronze-Age reminiscences and calls his comparison of Eutresis and Eleon in the Catalogue of Ships—the list of Greek contingents and places in *Iliad* 2 [[pache2020_homerguide]]—with Theban Linear B place names particularly cogent. She also explicitly considers Achilles’ account of Lesbos as Priam’s territory. But an intended picture of Anatolia before Greek migration is not, in her argument, an accurate survival of its political situation. After Hittite intervention at Miletus, architecture, pottery, and cult still indicate a Mycenaean identity; loss of political control did not entail disappearance of those inhabitants. Ephesus mattered in Mycenaean–Anatolian relations but is absent from the *Iliad*, an omission she tentatively relates to later rivalry with Miletus. Her objection concerns repeated reworking of cultural memory; it does not prove that no historical Achilles existed. [[bachvarova2016_hittite]]

The performance premise itself needs qualification. Amir Gilan, in Kelly and Metcalf’s edited volume (2021), calls many of Bachvarova’s proposed cultic settings conjectural because the evidence is fragmentary and circumstantial. Cult singing is well attested, but direct evidence for epic poets is sparse, and a text called a “song” does not by itself establish a particular performance. Gilan nevertheless identifies the Song of the Deeds of the Sea at the festival of Mount Hazzi as compelling evidence for narrative song in ritual and discusses further positive evidence. His criticism limits what can be reconstructed about performance; it is not a rejection of all Anatolian transmission. [[kelly2021_gods]]

Kullmann (2015) challenges a different part of West’s reconstruction: his proposed *Ur-Iliad*, an earlier version extending to Achilles’ death, from which the *Aethiopis* supposedly borrowed. Kullmann instead argues that the *Iliad* drew on the Memnon story, including Antilochus’ death, divine armour, and the weighing of heroes’ fates. His narrative evidence includes the weighing before Hector’s death: Zeus has already decided the outcome, and Hector lacks Achilles’ divine parentage. In the Memnon story, by contrast, both opponents have divine mothers. Kullmann also points to Antilochus’ characterization, chariot incidents, and the awkward archery contest as traces of that older story. He argues that West’s reversal requires unsupported changes to an earlier version of the poem. This dispute concerns the direction of narrative borrowing, not the Embassy’s dual forms. [[fantuzzi2015_greek]]

Lord’s *The Singer of Tales* (1960) adds a specific plot model. He reads the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* as stories of an absence that devastates those close to the absent hero, followed by a return that sets matters right. For Achilles, Patroclus enters battle as his double, wearing his armour, and returns the withdrawn warrior to the action by proxy. Lord also compares loss of a companion, disguise, contests, and renewed union with a woman. This is more specific than the general claim that Homer composed with traditional formulas.
